Hatvest: a hat that reads crop health

A conical field hat with a spectral sensor on the brim. Look at a plant, and the brim glows green if the leaf looks healthy or red if it may be stressed.

MSc Cleantech Innovation, Imperial College London · Handover 20 October 2026 · Group 4: Yuxuan Li, Xinrui (Rita) Geng, Imran (Rizki) Putranto, Wan Shuen Siaw · Project expert: to be confirmed · "Hatvest" is a working name.

In plain English

Hatvest is a farmer's hat that checks how healthy a plant is just by looking at it, and shows the answer as a green or red light on the brim.

How it works

Plants reflect light in two ways that matter here:

  • Healthy leaves soak up red light. The green stuff in leaves (chlorophyll) uses red light as food to grow, so very little red bounces back.
  • Healthy leaves bounce back lots of "invisible" light. Just beyond red is near-infrared light, which our eyes can't see. A healthy leaf's insides are full of tiny air pockets that scatter it back out, like a mirror maze.

When a plant struggles (too dry, too hot, hungry or sick), it loses some of its green stuff, so more red light bounces back. A small sensor on the front of the hat measures both kinds of light and turns them into one score, called NDVI. High score: healthy leaf, the light glows green. Low score: struggling leaf, it glows red.

Why it matters

  • Climate change is making droughts and heatwaves more common and more severe, and that is already hurting harvests.1
  • Most of the world's farms are tiny. About 84% are smaller than 2 hectares, roughly three football pitches.3 Those farmers can't afford drones or expensive equipment.
  • Many of these farmers already wear a conical hat in the field, so the sensor goes on something they already use and their hands stay free.

Why use it if a farmer can just look?

Honestly, our hat does not (yet) see problems before a farmer's eyes do; the research does not support that claim for this kind of sensor. What it does offer:

  1. A number instead of a guess. How green a leaf looks changes with sunshine, cloud and who is looking. A score is the same every time. IRRI, the international rice research institute, gave out over 250,000 colour cards to Asian rice farmers to make this judgement more consistent.[g] Our hat does the same job automatically.
  2. Spotting slow decline. Eyes catch obvious yellowing, but are worse at noticing that one corner of a field is slightly weaker than the rest.
  3. A record. Later versions could save each reading with its location to build a map of the whole field. Farmers who don't want that just use the light.
  4. No extra effort. It works while the farmer walks the field anyway.

The research behind it

What we claimWho showed it

What we found

We tested six plants on campus: three healthy and three struggling. Every healthy leaf scored higher than every struggling one, with a clear gap between the two groups.

What is not proven yet

  • It was a small test: six plants, one afternoon.
  • Changing light (sun, cloud, shade) throws readings off. For now we fix that by holding a sheet of white paper under the sensor first, which isn't practical in a field.
  • We haven't asked farmers whether they would wear it or find it useful.
  • Catching problems earlier than the eye is the big question for the next team. Our sensor already has extra colour channels to start testing it, and a cheap temperature sensor could be added.

In one line: Hatvest turns a farmer's "this plant looks a bit off" into a consistent score, hands-free, and is a first step towards spotting problems before anyone can see them.

1Summary

Climate change is making droughts and heatwaves more frequent and intense, so farmers need to catch crop stress earlier. Small-scale farmers usually spot it by eye, once leaves have already changed colour. Plant stress changes how leaves reflect red and near-infrared light, and a cheap spectral sensor can measure that. Research shows some spectral signals can flag stress before it is visible; whether a simple NDVI sensor can do that is still to be tested.

We built a working prototype: an AS7343 sensor on the front brim of a traditional conical hat, an Arduino Uno and 9 V battery at the back, and a red/green LED on the brim. It calculates NDVI and lights the LED. In our first test it separated healthy from stressed leaves with no overlap.

2Background: climate adaptation and crop stress

Why it matters now

  • Extremes are increasing. The IPCC finds that more frequent and intense weather extremes have already reduced food security, and that climate change has slowed the growth of agricultural productivity over the past 50 years.1
  • Heat costs yield. Each 1 °C of global warming is estimated to cut average yields of rice by about 3.2%, wheat by about 6% and maize by about 7.4%.2
  • Adaptation means acting sooner. Water, shade or treatment applied early can save a crop that would be lost if stress were only noticed once leaves wilt or yellow.

Who it is for

Small-scale farmers, especially in South-East Asia, and the agronomists and extension officers who advise them. Of the world's more than 608 million farms, about 84% are smaller than 2 hectares.3 Conical hats (caping, nón lá, salakot) are already standard field wear in the region, which is why we built the sensor into one: no new habit, and the farmer's hands stay free.

The problem

Farmers mostly check crops by eye. By the time leaves visibly change, the plant may already have lost yield. Tools that see stress earlier exist, but they are handheld, specialist, or work at a scale too coarse for small plots (see below).

Similar technologies already in use

Measuring plant health from red and near-infrared light is well established. What differs is the platform, the cost and who it is built for.

Value proposition: why use it if farmers can see stress?

Experienced farmers spot obvious stress by eye, and NDVI is not proven to see it earlier (see section 3). The honest selling point is different: turning what a farmer sees into a consistent, recorded number, hands-free, on a platform that can add earlier-warning sensors.

  1. Eyes are inconsistent; a number isn't. How green a leaf looks shifts with sun, cloud, time of day, crop variety and who is looking. IRRI's Leaf Colour Chart, given to over 250,000 Asian rice farmers, exists to standardise that "how green" judgement for fertiliser decisions.[g] Hatvest does the same job with a measured number, hands-free.
  2. Gradual decline is easy to miss. The eye catches obvious yellowing, but not one corner of a field being slightly less vigorous than the rest. Commercial NDVI tools such as GreenSeeker and Crop Circle are widely used this way, to check crop nitrogen status during the season rather than to detect disease early.[b] [d]
  3. Data, not memory. Eye checks leave no record. Logged readings (later with GPS) become a field map, a trend over time, and evidence for an agronomist, insurer or buyer.
  4. Coverage without extra effort. It measures while the farmer walks the field they already walk.

Points 2 to 4 are reasoning, not yet tested with farmers; validating them is part of the next phase.

3How NDVI works

A healthy leaf does two things with light:

  • It absorbs red light. Chlorophyll uses red light for photosynthesis, so little red is reflected.
  • It reflects near-infrared (NIR). Light the eye can't see, scattered back by the leaf's internal structure.

NDVI (Normalised Difference Vegetation Index)4,5 turns the contrast between the two into one number:

NDVI = (NIR − Red) ÷ (NIR + Red)

It runs from −1 to +1. Dense, green vegetation scores high; stressed, sparse or dead vegetation scores lower. In the literature NDVI mainly tracks the amount of green, photosynthetically active vegetation.5,6

Why red and NIR behave so differently

Two different physical processes are at work in the two bands.5,6

Red: absorbed by chlorophyll. Chlorophyll a and b absorb most strongly in the blue (around 430 to 450 nm) and the red (around 640 to 680 nm). A photon at those energies lifts an electron in the molecule to a higher energy state, and that energy feeds photosynthesis. So in the visible range a healthy leaf reflects little light; carotenoids absorb some blue too.

Green: absorbed less, so more of it comes back. Chlorophyll absorbs green only weakly, so more green light is scattered back out of the leaf than red or blue. That is why leaves look green. A leaf still absorbs much of the green light that hits it; it simply reflects more green than other visible colours.

NIR: scattered by the leaf's structure. Beyond about 700 nm chlorophyll stops absorbing, and the leaf has no strong absorption features up to about 1,300 nm. Inside, the spongy mesophyll is a loose arrangement of cells with air gaps between them. Each cell wall to air boundary is a sharp change in refractive index (roughly 1.4 to 1.5 for the wet cell wall against 1.0 for air), so it acts like a partial mirror. Light bounces between many such boundaries and, with almost nothing absorbing it, a large share (often around half) comes back out as reflected light; most of the rest passes through the leaf.

How stress changes the two bands

Stress effectRed bandNIR band
Chlorophyll breaks down (drought, nutrient shortage, disease, senescence)Less absorption, so higher red reflectance. This is the most consistent signal.Little direct effect
Leaf structure and water change (wilting, collapsing cells, damage)Little direct effectCan go either way. Wilting and lost leaf area usually lower NIR from a crop canopy, but a damaged single leaf can reflect more NIR: ozone-damaged leaves showed a raised NIR plateau.6

Our own data shows this. In the worked example below, the stressed leaf reflected more NIR than the healthy one (0.464 vs 0.422). Its NDVI fell because its red reflectance almost doubled (0.318 vs 0.174), not because NIR dropped. A rise in NIR on its own would push NDVI up; here the jump in red outweighed it. So the red band is doing most of the work.

Can NDVI catch stress before it is visible? Not reliably, on current evidence. A common claim is that chlorophyll changes before leaf structure does, so NDVI should flag stress early. But:

  • Red reflectance saturates. At medium to high chlorophyll levels, reflectance responds to chlorophyll changes more strongly near 550 nm (green) than near 675 nm (red), so small early losses barely move the red band.6 Tucker likewise found red radiance levels off sooner than NIR as green biomass increases.5 The red edge is a good indicator of leaf chlorophyll.6
  • The eye sees the same change. Chlorophyll loss in the visible range is what we see as yellowing.
  • NDVI has missed stress in field studies. It could not separate apple trees with different levels of mite attack, where pigment-based indices could,6 and it "did not differ significantly" between asymptomatic and symptomatic olive trees infected with Xylella; that early detection came from fluorescence, pigment and thermal traits.7

Our own test only compared leaves that were already visibly stressed. Treat "earlier than the eye" as a goal to test, not a result. Red-edge, green-band and thermal sensing are the strongest leads.

Why we calibrate with a white card

The sensor doesn't measure reflectance directly. It counts how much light arrives in each channel, and that count depends on two things besides the leaf: how much red and NIR the light source gives out (sunlight, cloud and lamps all have different colour balances) and how sensitive the sensor is in each channel. Neither is the same for red and NIR, so raw counts are biased before the leaf even comes into it.

A sheet of white paper reflects nearly all light in both channels equally. So a reading of white paper tells you "this is 100%" for each channel, in this light, with this sensor. Dividing the leaf reading by it turns counts into reflectance: the fraction of the available light the leaf sends back, like an exam mark "out of" a total. That is what the w command stores.

Worked example (real readings from our test)

Step 1. White reference (white paper, outdoors, 19 Oct): red = 2,454 counts, NIR = 3,613 counts. Even on white paper NIR reads higher than red, which shows the light and sensor are not balanced between channels.

Step 2. Divide each leaf reading by the white reference to get reflectance:

Red countsRed reflectanceNIR countsNIR reflectance
Healthy leaf (H1)427427 ÷ 2,454 = 0.1741,5241,524 ÷ 3,613 = 0.422
Stressed leaf (S1)781781 ÷ 2,454 = 0.3181,6761,676 ÷ 3,613 = 0.464

The healthy leaf reflects 17% of the red light (chlorophyll absorbs the rest) and 42% of the NIR. The stressed leaf reflects almost twice as much red (32%), because it has less chlorophyll.

Step 3. NDVI from reflectance:

Healthy:  (0.422 − 0.174) ÷ (0.422 + 0.174) = 0.248 ÷ 0.596 = 0.416  → green (≥ 0.35)
Stressed: (0.464 − 0.318) ÷ (0.464 + 0.318) = 0.146 ÷ 0.782 = 0.186  → red   (< 0.35)

These match the values the Arduino logged (0.416 and 0.186).

What goes wrong without the white card

Put the raw counts straight into the formula and you get:

Healthy:  (1,524 − 427) ÷ (1,524 + 427) = 0.562
Stressed: (1,676 − 781) ÷ (1,676 + 781) = 0.364  → would wrongly glow green

Both numbers are inflated because the light and sensor favour NIR, and the stressed leaf ends up above the 0.35 threshold. The white card removes that bias. Note that a simple change in brightness (the same light, just dimmer) would cancel out in the NDVI ratio anyway; what the white card really corrects is the balance between red and NIR, and that balance is what changes with sun, cloud and lamps.

The catch: the white reference is only valid for the light at the moment it was taken. Clouds, shade, time of day or moving indoors all change it. It cannot rescue light sources with almost no NIR, such as indoor LED lighting. This is the main thing for the next team to solve (see section 8).

4The hat we built

How a farmer uses it

  1. Wear the hat and walk the field.
  2. Look at a plant and hold still.
  3. The brim sensor reads the leaf (about once a second).
  4. The brim LED glows green (NDVI ≥ 0.35) or red (below).

5Firmware and logger

Arduino sketch

File: firmware/hatvest_ndvi/hatvest_ndvi.ino. Needs the Adafruit AS7343 library.

  • Start-up: LED self-test (green, then red). If the sensor is missing, the LED flashes red continuously.
  • Sensor settings: gain 64×, ATIME 29, ASTEP 599 (about 50 ms integration).
  • Calibration: w stores the white reference and saves it to EEPROM, so it survives power cycles. It is loaded automatically on start-up.
  • Measuring: streams a reading every second; r takes one reading; s pauses or resumes the stream.
  • Maths: reflectance = leaf ÷ white for each channel, then NDVI = (NIR − Red) ÷ (NIR + Red). NDRE = (NIR − Edge) ÷ (NIR + Edge) is printed too.
  • Safety checks: saturated readings (65,535) are rejected with a warning; invalid maths turns the LED off.
  • Threshold: NDVI_THRESHOLD = 0.35, set from our test data.

Example Serial Monitor output (115200 baud):

SAMPLE raw red(F7)=427 edge(F8)=419 nir=1524 | NDVI=0.416 NDRE=0.147 -> GREEN (healthy)

Python logger

File: tools/ndvi_logger.py. Saves each reading with a timestamp and a label to ndvi_log.csv next to the script.

pip install pyserial
python tools/ndvi_logger.py          # finds the port automatically
> label healthy leaf 1
> w                                  # white card under the sensor first
> r                                  # one reading, saved to CSV
> q

Close the Arduino Serial Monitor first: only one program can use the port. The logger pauses the live stream so only deliberate readings are saved.

6Testing and results

Method (19 October 2026)

  • Outdoors in daylight, on campus. White reference taken before each set.
  • Six plants: three healthy (H1 to H3) and three with visible stress (S1 to S3), chosen by eye.
  • Three to five readings per leaf, 20 readings in total, logged with labels.
GroupReadingsMean NDVIRange
Healthy90.430.386 – 0.498
Stressed110.260.186 – 0.313

What we learned

  • NDVI separated the groups with no overlap, using parts that cost far less than a commercial meter.
  • Repeatable: readings on the same leaf varied by about ±0.02.
  • Threshold: 0.35 classified all 20 readings correctly. The original 0.25 missed 7 of 11 stressed readings, so we changed the sketch.
  • NDRE did not help here: the red-edge index did not separate healthy from stressed leaves.
  • Light level: the first two white references (NIR counts 10 to 25) were far too dark and are invalid. Even the valid ones (about 2,300 to 3,600 counts) were below the recommended 5,000 to 50,000, so raise the gain next time.

Caveats: one session, six campus plants chosen by eye, autumn conditions. Some "stressed" leaves may have been natural autumn senescence rather than crop stress. The 0.35 threshold is provisional and should be re-derived from larger, crop-specific samples.

Raw data: data/ndvi_log_2026-10-19.csv (columns: timestamp, type, label, red_F7, edge_F8, nir, ndvi, ndre).

7Limitations

  • Calibration depends on the light at that moment. Retake the white reference whenever light changes. Readings under indoor LED lighting are meaningless.
  • Electronics exposed. The 3D-printed enclosures need resizing and fitting.
  • Balance. The Uno and battery at the back make the hat tip backwards.
  • Sensor board LED. The AS7343 board's own indicator LED may reach the sensor at close range; not yet tested.
  • Narrow validation. Campus plants only, no crops under controlled stress, no farmer testing.
  • Non-green plants. Healthy red or purple (anthocyanin-rich) leaves might read as stressed. In purple sweet potato leaves, red-edge reflectance still tracked chlorophyll whether or not anthocyanins were present, while green-band estimates were distorted.8 That study did not test NDVI, and neither have we.
  • Aiming and distance are left to the wearer; both affect the reading.

9Your first week

  1. Get it running on the bench. Install the Arduino IDE and the Adafruit AS7343 library, upload the sketch, check the LED self-test.
  2. Repeat our test outdoors with docs/bench_test_guide.html and the logger. Do the gain check first (white card counts between about 5,000 and 50,000).
  3. Decide the light strategy: sky sensor or active light. This shapes the hardware, the enclosure and the budget.
  4. Plan a proper trial: which crop, how you will create controlled stress (e.g. withholding water), how many plants, how often.
  5. Fix the physical basics: enclosures, harness and balance, so the hat can be worn for longer tests.
